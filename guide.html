What the explorer shows, where every number comes from, how the data is handled and derived, why it's built the way it is, and how to get the most out of it, with short lessons on the weather behind it.

1. Quick start

  1. Pick a region in the View tab: Maui – Hawaiʻi Island or the Salish Sea. The newest complete model run loads by itself as "Surface wind & gusts".
  2. Read the colours with the key on the map (bottom left; on a phone, under the title). Wind uses the University of Washington WRF wind bands in 5-knot steps.
  3. Hover (or tap on a phone) anywhere for the model's wind, gust and height at that point.
  4. Play the forecast with ▶ in the time bar, or step with ← → (15 min; Shift for an hour). Space plays and pauses.
  5. Try a preset in the View tab (under the view list), such as "Gap flow: along the channel". Each one answers a question and sets the layers for you.
  6. For smooth playback, press Download whole run once and keep looking while it fetches every hour in the background.
  7. Check the model against reality: turn on observed wind at stations, the Satellite, or the balloon comparison in the Inversion box.

Drag to rotate, right-drag or Shift-drag to pan, and scroll or pinch to zoom. H hides the controls; 1–9 jump to the first nine views in the view list.

2. What it is, and why

A 3D explorer of forecast wind through the whole lower atmosphere over two marine areas with strong terrain-forced winds, the ʻAlenuihāhā Channel and the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and the waters around them. The end goal is to see the wind as a volume: how it's steered by mountains, squeezed through gaps, capped by inversions, and made gusty or turbulent. Flat wind maps show one level at a time.

Design choices

  • Straight from the source. Your browser reads NOAA's model files directly from NOAA's public cloud buckets. No server sits in between, so there's nothing to maintain or pay for, and the data is as fresh as NOAA's.
  • Only what's on screen is fetched. A whole model run is gigabytes; the page reads just the fields, levels and area it needs (a few MB to tens of MB per forecast hour) and fetches more only when you turn something on.
  • One volume per hour. Everything you see (slice, cross-section, particles, barbs, readouts) samples from the same assembled 3D column of each model grid point, so the displays always agree.
  • Honest gaps. Where the model has no data (below its ground, or above the loaded top), the display shows a hole, not a guess. Where a number is an estimate or a proxy, it says so.
  • Presets over knobs. There are many settings, so presets set sensible combinations for common questions, and the controls are grouped into tabs, with the rarely changed ones in "Data".

Swell (NOAA wave model)

GFS-Wave (NOAA's WAVEWATCH III), read in the browser from NOAA's public cloud bucket. The Salish Sea uses the West Coast grid and Hawaiʻi the eastern Pacific grid, both 1/6° (about 18 km), in runs every 6 hours with hourly output. Per point it gives the total significant wave height, the wave model's own wind sea, and up to three swell trains, each with height, period and direction.

The files are compressed with JPEG2000, so the explorer loads the open-source OpenJPEG decoder (as WebAssembly) the first time you turn on Sea state. Land and sheltered waters have no values: in the model, no swell reaches the inner Strait of Juan de Fuca, the San Juans or Puget Sound, which is broadly true.

Places (Hawaiʻi)

Fifty named places from the owner's notes for the ʻAlenuihāhā crossing (Honokōhau Harbor north to Nishimura Bay, across to ʻĀhihi Bay and back): waypoints, fish aggregating devices (FADs), lights and buoys, harbours and anchorages, points, peaks, airports, channels and islands. Each has its position, the meaning of its name, light characteristics and navigation notes. Place-name meanings: Ulukau's Hawaiian place names and manomano.io. Converted by pipeline/places.py, which also fixed two navaids at Honokōhau listed under Maui and one typo, and labels "Waimea Valley" as Waimea Saddle (the gap between Kohala and Mauna Kea, where the trades squeeze through toward Kawaihae).

NWS zone forecasts

The National Weather Service's coastal waters forecast text, read live from its public API (api.weather.gov): Honolulu's for the Hawaiʻi zones (PHZ116–124, including PHZ121 ʻAlenuihāhā Channel) and Seattle's for the Washington zones (the Strait of Juan de Fuca, the San Juans, Admiralty Inlet, Puget Sound and the outer coast). The zone outlines come from the same API, stored with the site. Canadian waters (Environment Canada's marine forecasts) aren't included yet.

Fetch (for the waves)

For every water point on a 1 km grid, and for 16 wind directions: how far the wind has blown over open water to get there, measured back to the first land along the GSHHG coastline. Computed once, offline, from the coastline. Where the line leaves the map over water (the Pacific, the Strait of Georgia), the fetch is the distance to the edge plus 300 km, capped at 600 km.

Live satellite

GOES-West (the NOAA geostationary satellite over the Pacific), from NASA GIBS, a public image service a web page can read. A new image comes every 10 minutes, and GIBS posts it about 20–40 minutes later. Three products:

  • GeoColor: true colour by day; at night, clouds from infrared over a dark map with city lights.
  • Clean infrared (band 13): the temperature of whatever the satellite sees. Cold cloud tops look bright, day and night. Clear sky is left see-through, so only clouds show.
  • Visible (band 2): reflected sunlight at 1 km; dark at night.

5. How the data is handled

Finding the run

By default the explorer shows the newest complete run: the latest run whose files through the needed hour have all been posted. Models post hour by hour over roughly an hour or two, so the newest folder is often still filling. You can instead choose "Newest long run · 0–48 h" or "Newest run, still posting". If a newer run is on its way, the status says so; if the run looks older than it should, it's flagged stale.

Which run is on screen is always named: the title bar shows the model, run time and length (e.g. "HRRR 03Z · 0–18 h"), and the status line says the same with the run's age.

Staying fresh: the explorer never switches runs on its own, so the view doesn't change while you're reading it. When you come back to the tab after 10 minutes or more away, and every 15 minutes while it's visible, it checks for a newer complete run. If there is one, it offers it with a Load it button. Station observations refresh every 10 minutes, but only while the tab is visible; coming back after longer fetches them straight away. Nothing is fetched while the tab is hidden, and animation pauses.

Fetching only what's needed

  1. Each model file comes with a small index listing where every field starts and ends. The page reads the index, then fetches just the byte ranges of the fields it needs, merging neighbours into fewer requests.
  2. Each field is a compressed GRIB2 message covering the model's whole domain (all of the continental US for HRRR). It's decoded in background workers and cropped to the region.
  3. On HRRR's Lambert grid, winds come relative to the grid; they're turned to true north (about 16° over Puget Sound). HRRR's vertical motion ω (Pa/s) is converted to w (m/s) with w = −ω R T / (p g).

Just in time

Every hour loads a core set: the surface fields, the above-ground winds, and wind, height, temperature and humidity from 1000 to 850 mb, which covers the surface layer, the marine layer and fog. Everything else comes on demand: a slice aloft, a cross-section, particles through the whole column, the inversion, turbulence or the channel analysis each ask for the levels and fields they need, and only the missing ones are fetched. The status line shows what's loaded and its size (on HRRR the core set is about 31 MB an hour). The next hours prefetch in the background.

Downloaded once per run. Each forecast hour's fields are downloaded once and kept (cropped to the region: about 2 MB an hour, against about 30 MB downloaded on HRRR, whose files cover the whole US). A second pass through the forecast, or going back to an earlier hour, downloads nothing. The memory budget is about 400 MB on a computer and 120 MB on a phone, which covers a full run. Up to 8 hours are also kept as built 3D volumes; others rebuild from the kept fields in a fraction of a second. The status line shows how many hours are downloaded and the memory they use.

Download whole run (under Load) fetches every hour of the run in the background, two at a time, while you look at the first one. It fetches what the layers on screen need, plus the 15-minute surface data, so playback is smooth from the start. On the Salish Sea an 18-hour HRRR run took about 80 seconds (about 500 MB downloaded, 35 MB kept). Click it again to stop; it also stops if the run changes. Turning on more layers afterwards fetches only the new fields.

Building the volume

For each grid point the explorer builds one column: the model's terrain, 2 m temperature and humidity, the above-ground winds, and every pressure level at its real height. Pressure levels at or below the model's ground are dropped (models fill them in by extrapolation, and those values aren't real).

  • Between heights: linear in height.
  • Below the lowest value: wind, temperature and humidity are held down to the ground; vertical velocity and vorticity are left undefined there, because they only exist on pressure levels.
  • Between grid points: bilinear. Colours are then chosen pixel by pixel from the interpolated value, so colour-band edges are smooth contours.
  • Between hours: a linear blend of the two hours around the time on screen, so playback is smooth. With 15-minute data, the surface wind blends between 15-minute steps. Classified layers (inversion, turbulence dots, Froude colouring) use the nearer whole hour rather than blending categories.

Heights and units

Heights are shown three ways: ≈ mb · m · ft. The pressure is interpolated (in log pressure) between the domain-average heights of the model's levels, so it's approximate, and for heights above ground it's marked "over water". Wind is computed in knots throughout. Display units (kt, mph, m/s, km/h) change only the numbers written: the colour bands stay knot bands, and barbs are always in knots.

The inversion detector

In each column, between 950 and 600 mb, it finds the strongest increase of potential temperature with height, then grows the layer up and down while θ keeps rising faster than 5 K/km. That gives the layer's base and top. For display, each column's result is replaced by the median of its 5 × 5 neighbourhood where at least 60% found one, which removes speckle.

Strength (TII, 0–10) is scored exactly as the Alenuihāhā weather briefing scores the Hilo balloon, so the numbers mean the same thing on both. It combines the potential-temperature jump (scale 2.5 K), the actual temperature rise (2.4 °C), the humidity drop (45%) and the thickness (thinner is sharper; scale 300 m):

TII = 10 · tanh( (Δθ/2.5 · ΔT/2.4 · ΔRH/45) / (thickness/300 + 0.4) )

Strong is 6 or more, moderate 3–6, weak under 3 (the briefing's thresholds). A layer where the temperature doesn't actually rise scores 0: it's very stable but not a true inversion. The model's levels are about 250 m apart, which smooths sharp inversions, so many of the layers it finds score low. The inversion box says what share are true inversions and gives the strongest value.

At the balloon site (Hilo in Hawaiʻi, Quillayute on the Salish Sea), the inversion box also gives the model's own single-column inversion, found and scored like a sounding. In Hawaiʻi it adds the briefing's gap-wind flag:

  • HIGH: strong inversion with 850–925 mb trades of 30 kt or more;
  • MED: strong with 25 kt or more, moderate with 30 kt or more, or moderate with 23 kt or more;
  • LOW: otherwise.

The trade speed is the 75th percentile of the winds at the model's levels from 850 to 925 mb.

The latest balloon for that site comes from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive, which a web page can read. The balloon is run through the same detector and score, and the box compares it with the model at the launch hour (or the run's nearest hour, saying how far off it is). The verdict is agree or disagree on whether there's an inversion, the difference in base height, and both strength scores.

If IEM's newest sounding is more than two days old, the box gives its date and says it's too old to compare. On 2026-09-27, Quillayute was current but IEM's Hilo copy was 8 days behind. The University of Wyoming has current Hilo soundings but doesn't allow browser access, so fresh Hilo comparisons would need a small scheduled job; that's not set up.

Note that HRRR's default column (every 50 mb) is coarse for a thin inversion. Choose the 25 mb column in the Data tab for a fairer comparison on the Salish Sea.

Checked against 28 Hilo balloon soundings (the 12-hour forecast): it agrees on whether there's an inversion 64% of the time (it misses some that the balloon sees), and its base runs about 200 m low. Treat the band as indicative.

Clouds

These are the model's own cloud: its cloud-water and cloud-ice amounts (g/kg), which its physics produces where air saturates. They're added together and interpolated in height at each grid point, then resampled onto a regular grid over the map (about 1.5 km) at 48 heights up to 8 km. They're drawn as stacked see-through sheets, brighter where the cloud is denser.

These are display choices, not model data:

  • anything under 0.005 g/kg is treated as clear;
  • about 0.6 g/kg looks like a dense cloud;
  • the grey-to-white shading with height is for looks only.

Rain, snow and hail aren't included. The cloud note gives cloud cover and the typical base and top, counting cloud of 0.02 g/kg or more.

Chop (local wind waves)

Wind waves grow with wind speed, with the distance the wind has blown over the water (fetch), and with how long it has blown (duration). The explorer uses the standard growth relations from the US Army Corps of Engineers' Coastal Engineering Manual (JONSWAP-based, deep water):

u*² = C_D U10², C_D = 0.001 (1.1 + 0.035 U10) Hm0 = 0.0413 · (u*²/g) · (g F / u*²)^½ Tp = 0.751 · (u*/g) · (g F / u*²)^⅓ duration limit: g F_e / u*² = 5.23e-3 · (g t / u*)^1.5, use the smaller of F and F_e fully developed caps: g Hm0/u*² ≤ 211.5, g Tp/u* ≤ 239.8

Inputs:

  • U10: the model's 10 m wind at the point (the 15-minute data when it's on).
  • F: the fetch in the direction the wind comes from.
  • t: "wind for" in the Sea state box (default 12 hours).

Hm0 is the significant wave height (roughly the average of the highest third of waves); Tp is the peak period. As a check, 10 m/s (19 kt) over 50 km gives 1.1 m (3.7 ft) at 4.4 s. On the Salish Sea on 2026-09-27, the outer coast reached 8.5 ft under unlimited duration, and 4.4 ft with 12 hours, which is more believable.

Combining chop and swell

Wave energies add, so heights combine as the square root of the sum of squares: total = √(chop² + swell₁² + swell₂² + swell₃²). "Show" in the Sea state box picks the total, chop only or swell only. On the map, swell is blended smoothly between the wave model's 18 km cells. The readout and the inspector use the nearest sea cell's own swell trains.

The surface inspector

Click the water with Sea state on for a true-scale 200 m square of sea at that point: no exaggeration, real-time motion. A stylised boat gives scale: a Cape George 31 cutter in Hawaiʻi and a Pacific Seacraft 37 on the Salish Sea, from their published length, beam and draft. They're sailing a close reach and heel with the wind; mast heights are approximate and the hull lines simplified. The sea is built from 56 wave trains: 32 for the chop and 8 for each of up to three swells (narrow in period and within about ±12° of direction). For the chop's trains:

  • Periods: drawn from a JONSWAP wave spectrum around the peak period (peak enhancement 3.3).
  • Directions: spread about the wind direction.
  • Phases: random.
  • Height: scaled so their significant height equals the chop's.
  • Speeds: each moves at its deep-water speed (ω² = gk).

Gusts in the inspector: the model's gust at the spot (its estimate of the peak gusts, next to the mean wind) drives puffs and lulls drifting downwind across the patch at about the wind speed; the strongest reach the model's gust. Puffs darken and roughen the water like cat's paws; lulls go glassier. The boat heels to the wind where it is, easing into each puff over about a second, and the readout at the top left shows the wind at that moment ("Wind now … · mean … · gusts to …", highlighted during a puff). The strength follows the model; the timing, size and spacing of puffs are illustrative (the model doesn't simulate individual gusts, which last seconds and span tens to hundreds of metres).

Sailing in the inspector: choose the tack (Port or Starboard) and the point of sail (close-hauled 48°, close reach 60°, beam reach 90°, broad reach 135°, running 170° true wind angle). The boat takes up that heading against the model's wind, and its sails are trimmed to the apparent wind.

  • Boat speed comes from approximate polars for the boat (Cape George 31 in Hawaiʻi, Pacific Seacraft 37 on the Salish Sea). These are estimates for heavy full-keel cutters from their waterline length (hull speed about 6.8 and 7.1 kn), not measured polars. Speed eases up and down with the puffs over several seconds.
  • Where the wind comes from: the model selected, at the time on the slider: the mean is the model's 10 m wind at the spot (blended between hours), the gust is the model's gust field (HRRR's 15-minute data when loaded, else the hour's). Puffs and lulls are simulated between those.
  • Instruments: HDG (heading), STW (speed through the water, i.e. boat speed), TWS, TWD and TWA (true wind speed, direction and angle, in the wind over the moving water), AWS and AWA (apparent wind), heel, COG and SOG (course and speed over the ground: through the water plus the current's set and drift), leeway, Current (drift in kt and the direction it sets toward; "–" when currents aren't applied), Current effect (the current along the boat's course, + fair in green or − foul in red, and how many degrees it sets the track off the course through the water, to port or starboard), VMG (over the ground, toward the wind ↑ or away from it ↓) and the log. P and S say which side the wind is on. Below them: the sails set, and the local time with the sun and moon.
  • Seas at a glance (above the instruments): the combined height, the chop and each swell (height, period, direction), and how much boat speed the waves cost on this heading. The full wave notes stay in "Details".
  • Sails and reefing (the Sails menu; auto by default). Each boat has its own wardrobe:
    • Cape George 31: main with three reefs, yankee and a smaller yankee, staysail, storm staysail, trysail, spinnaker. Reefing sequence: full main, yankee and staysail → 1 reef → 1 reef with the small yankee → 2 reefs and staysail → 3 reefs and staysail → 3 reefs and storm staysail → trysail and storm staysail.
    • Pacific Seacraft 37: main with two reefs in the Port Townsend Sails style (the first a little deeper than usual, the second like a third reef), furling 115% genoa, staysail, storm staysail, trysail, asymmetric spinnaker. Sequence: full main and genoa (staysail added on a reach) → genoa rolled to ~90% → 1 reef and genoa ~90% → 1 reef and staysail → 2 reefs and staysail → 2 reefs and storm staysail → trysail and storm staysail.
    Auto picks the fullest plan that keeps the heel at or under about 20° in the model's gusts (the apparent wind in a gust, at the sails' height), and never less reefed than a floor by gust strength (for running, where the heel says little): Cape George 1 reef from 22 kt gusts, then 26, 30, 35, 42, trysail from 50; Pacific Seacraft genoa rolled from 20, reef from 23, then 28, 33, 42, trysail from 50. Off the wind (from ~95–100°) in gusts up to 15–16 kt it flies the spinnaker. Choose a plan yourself to see the difference: more sail than auto is faster until the heel passes ~25°, then slower; less sail is slower. The staysail comes down from a broad reach when a bigger headsail flies (the main blankets it). Storm sails are drawn orange. Reef points and thresholds are reasonable cruising choices, not the owners' own rules.
  • Heel is worked out, not looked up: the sails' heeling force (their area, the apparent wind at their height and a side-force coefficient that is highest close-hauled and small running) times the height of their centre of effort above the keel, balanced against the boat's righting moment (approximate displacement and stability: Cape George ~7,900 kg, Pacific Seacraft ~7,350 kg). Puffs heel it more; reefing brings it back.
  • Leeway: the boat slips sideways more when heeled and slow (the usual rule of thumb, leeway ≈ K·heel/speed², capped at 10°), typically 4–6° close-hauled, near 0 running. The boat and its track on the map move along the COG. No current: tidal streams aren't in the model.
  • Speed in waves: the polar speed is reduced by the waves: in proportion to wave height squared over the waterline length, full in head seas, a quarter abeam, nothing from astern (about 10% close-reaching in 5 ft chop, 25% or so bashing into 2 m seas).
  • Sun, moon and sky for the time on screen at the spot: the sun's position lights the boat, land and sea; the sky goes through day, sunset or sunrise colours (warm toward the sun), civil, nautical and astronomical twilight to night with stars; the moon is in its real place and phase (drawn about 4× its real size so it reads), lights a moonlit night and glints on the water toward it. The clock runs on in real time from the slider time; the time is shown in local time and UTC in the wind box on the view and on the sun and moon line.
  • Night lights (the "night" menu, shown once it's dark): natural (starlight and moonlight only), brighter (lifts the sea and boat like night vision, sky left dark, so the surface reads) or spotlight (a searchlight from the bow onto the water ahead, lit by the wave slopes so the chop's shape shows, plus a deck floodlight on the boat).
  • Terrain exaggeration (terrain 1×, 2×, 3×, 5×): stretches the heights of the land around the spot; the sea, waves and boat stay true scale.
  • Wind history: a trace of the last 1, 2 or 5 minutes, like a wind-history instrument page: true wind speed (blue) against the model's mean and gust (dashed), with apparent wind (orange) and boat speed (white). The simulated wind never goes above the model's gust. There's no direction trace: the simulated puffs don't shift the wind direction (the model gives no basis for that).
  • Compass (bottom right, north up): the boat's heading, the true wind (blue) and the apparent wind (orange), each arrow pointing from where the wind comes.
  • Motion in the waves: the water is sampled at the bow, stern and both sides, so waves shorter than the hull mostly cancel out, as they do for a real boat. The boat then heaves, pitches and rolls toward that water surface at its own natural rhythm (heave ~2.4 s, pitch ~2.6 s, roll ~3.6 s, roll lightly damped), so it lags the waves, overshoots a little and keeps rolling after a big one. The roll adds to the sailing heel, and is damped much more when the sails are drawing (a boat under sail rolls far less than one motoring). The waves' orbital flow also pushes bow and stern sideways by different amounts, so the boat yaws a few degrees about its course and is steered back (most in quartering and following seas). It's a reasonable feel for a heavy cruiser, not a seakeeping model: there's no speed lost in waves, no leeway or current, no surfing, no slamming, and heel is a set curve against the apparent wind, not worked out from sail force and stability.
  • Surroundings: the real land within about 30 km at true scale (the map's ground heights), and the sea out to the horizon, fading into distance haze. The view starts behind the boat looking along its heading; drag to look around.
  • Making way: the boat moves at its polar speed, so nearby coast slides past and the waves, puffs and wind streaks move relative to it (the streaks show the apparent wind). The Log counts the distance; Reset position puts the boat back on the clicked spot. It doesn't steer around land.
  • Trim: sails trimmed to the apparent wind (eased out as you bear away); under trysail the boom is lashed amidships.
  • View: Reset view goes back to the chase view behind the boat; Sail view closes in from leeward, side-on to the apparent wind at mid-rig height, the best place to watch the flow round the sails. Drag to look around from either. Cockpit puts you at the helm on the windward side, carried by the boat: the horizon heels, rolls, pitches, yaws and heaves as it would; choose where to look: ahead, to leeward, to windward, up at the sails (from the leeward corner of the cockpit with a wide lens, the whole rig), at the headsails (from the leeward rail by the mast, to watch their telltales) or astern. A Motion readout shows the heel from the sails, the roll from the waves and the total, pitch (bow up or down), yaw off the course, heave, and the vertical g you'd feel.
  • Size and place: drag the viewer by its title bar to move it anywhere; drag its bottom-right corner to resize it (the corner follows the pointer); ▁ minimises it to its title bar (▢ restores it), so it doesn't cover the map while you follow a route. It opens where you left it, at the size you left it. On a narrow screen it fills the width under the top bar.
  • Weather (Controls → Sea & scene, on): the model's weather at the spot and time (HRRR, or RRFS in Hawaiʻi; in a passage replay, the passage's weather at the boat): cloud as a deck overhead drifting with the wind (scattered cells when thin, a full grey deck when overcast) that greys the sky and dims the sun, its glint on the water, the stars and the moon; fog closing the view in to the model's visibility (under ~1 nm the sky itself is the fog); rain falling at its rate (drizzle, light rain, rain, heavy) and blown by the wind; lightning flashes in thunderstorms. The Data tab gives the air temperature, dewpoint, visibility, rain, cloud and ceiling and the source. Loaded once for the run's hours round the spot (a few MB) the first time the viewer opens there.
  • Layout: the view and the time slider stay at the top; below them two tabs: Data (seas, instruments, sails, wind history, details: the default) and Controls, grouped under Sailing (tack, point of sail, sails, passage speed, reset position), Wind (streaks, density, flow round the sails), Sea & scene (Beaufort texture, spray & wake, satellite, terrain exaggeration, night lights) and View (reset, sail view, cockpit and where to look). Only the tab's contents scroll, so the tabs never go out of reach, and the panel keeps its height (in full screen too). It widens with the window (to 620 px); the sun and moon ride on the time line.
  • Full screen: ⛶ in the inspector's header; Esc or ⤡ to leave.
  • Wind particles (on by default, can be switched off; a density slider from 200 to 6,000 streaks, 1,200 to start): streaks from just above the water (0.3 m) to about 30 m, spread so there are as many near the surface as higher up. Each moves at the wind at its height and is carried through the puffs and lulls. The model gives the 10 m wind; other heights use the standard neutral log profile over the sea, with the roughness from the Charnock relation (rougher sea in stronger wind). In 20 kt that's about 73% of the 10 m wind at 0.5 m, 85% at 2 m (deck height), 106% at 20 m and 110% at 30 m (masthead is ~13–15 m). The profile assumes neutral air: over cool water under warm air (common on the Salish Sea) the real wind drops off faster near the water; over warm water, less. Brightness shows force: the wind's push on a sail goes with its dynamic pressure, ½ρV², speed squared, so each streak's brightness follows V² against the mean wind: a puff 20% stronger glows about 44% brighter and warms in colour; lulls fade. The spacing of the streaks stays even, as the air's own density does (it doesn't bunch up in a gust, it moves faster).
  • Model time (the slider under the view, with ▶ to play: the wind and sea here as the forecast goes on): the forecast hours of the loaded run, labelled in local time and UTC with the lead time and run; it moves the main time slider too, and the viewer redraws for each hour. While the viewer sails a planned passage the same row is the passage: drag it to move the boat along the route, ▶ to sail it from there, ⏸ to pause; it goes back to the forecast when the replay ends.
  • Tidal current (where there's a NOAA prediction station within 5 km, with Tidal currents → Current effects on waves on; in a passage replay planned with currents, always, from the planner's current at the boat's position and time): the waves meet the current as they would (each wave keeps its frequency over the ground, so against the current it's shortened and steepened, and breaks if the current is strong enough to stop it; with the current it lengthens and flattens); the boat sails in the wind over the moving water (what its instruments read, stronger with the wind against the tide); and it drifts with the current, so COG and SOG (over the ground) differ from heading and boat speed. Under the seas summary, the Current line gives its drift and set, the nearest station, and On the waves: for the chop and each swell, whether it runs against or with the current, how much higher or lower, how far apart the waves are instead of in still water, and whether the current blocks it (a short, breaking sea). Without currents applied it says so and how to turn them on.
  • Flow round the sails (on by default): the sails set (reefed or full main, trysail, whichever headsails are flying, the spinnaker) each carry lift, and the air near the boat bends round them. Each sail is modelled the standard way for lifting surfaces (lifting-line theory): cut into strips up its luff, each strip a vortex along its quarter chord with trailing vortices shed from the leech downwind, of strength ½·V·c·CL (the apparent wind at that height, the strip's chord, the lift for the angle). The streaks take the velocity those vortices induce: lifted ahead of the luff, about 10% faster just to leeward and slower to windward, bent through the slot between the headsails and main, turned down off the leech, with tip vortices at the head and foot. From about 105° apparent the eased sails start to stall; running they're mostly separated, pushing by drag, and the streaks slow and churn in each sail's lee. The flow round the sails has its own streaks, in amber to pale gold with bright heads and short fading trails (so their curving paths show): released in a band just upwind of the sails and across their span, amber where the flow slows (to windward, in a stalled lee), pale gold to white where it speeds up (to leeward, through the slot); their number follows the density slider (a third of it). The background wind streaks carry on as before (a little dimmer while these are on). Half of these streaks start right at a sail's luff, just off the cloth on either side, so they run along the sail to the leech: near a sail the air can't pass through it, so the flow turns to run along it (attached), faster on the leeward side than the windward (the lift); where the sail stalls, the leeward flow breaks away and churns instead. It shows the pattern of the flow, not a CFD solution (flat sails, no twist, mast wake or the hull's own flow).
  • Telltales (shown twice real size so they read): three pairs on each headsail's luff (15% of the chord back, both sides: red on the port side, green on starboard), two on each headsail's leech and three on the main's leech. Streaming aft: well trimmed. When the apparent wind comes forward of the trim (pinching or a header) the windward ones lift and flutter; when it goes aft of it (bearing away, or a puff) or the sails stall off the wind, the leeward ones stall and curl; the leech telltales curl round to leeward when the leech stalls. The trim is set for the apparent wind when you choose the course, so the puffs and lulls work the telltales as they would. The Sails line reads them out: streaming, windward lifting (bear away or ease) or leeward stalling (head up or trim in).
  • Sail force (instruments): drive (forward) and heeling force in kgf for the sails set, from the same lift and drag, with whether the flow is attached, partly separated or separated. Reef and watch both fall; bear away and watch drive rise while heeling force drops.
  • Beaufort texture (on by default): the sea looks the way the wind makes it. Whitecaps cover the share of the sea the wind gives (Monahan's relation: ~0.1% in 10 kt, ~1% in 20 kt, ~4% in 30 kt, ~10% in 40 kt; more under the puffs), stretched along the crests and forming and fading as they drift; from Force 7 foam is blown in streaks along the wind; from Force 8 spindrift haze off the crests. The Seas line gives the Beaufort force and the open sea's description (in short fetch the waves are smaller than the description, the whitecaps and streaks much the same).
  • Spray & wake (on by default): the bow wave and a wake (a turbulent strip astern and the Kelvin wedge's arms at about 19.5°) whiten more the faster the boat goes; surfing throws sheets of spray from the bow; a slam throws a burst of spray whose size follows how hard the bow came down (1 at the slam threshold, up to 3×), with random droplets that rise, fall and blow downwind, and leaves a patch of foam. Nothing is scripted: they happen when the simulated motion does it.
  • Surfing: a wave's face pushes the boat on down it (gravity along the water's slope under the hull, about half effective on a heavy hull, the surge dying away over ~3 s). When that carries it past hull speed the STW box turns green and "Surfing" shows; Max STW keeps the last minute's best. Heavy full-keel cutters surge more than they truly surf, and this reproduces that: it takes steep following seas.
  • Slamming: the forefoot lifts clear of a wave and comes down fast. When it re-enters faster than the usual threshold for a slam (0.093·√(g·L), Ochi's criterion, ~0.8 m/s for these boats), "Slam!" flashes red, the bow is knocked up and a little speed is lost; the instruments count slams over 5 minutes. Most likely close-hauled into short steep seas, especially wind against current.
  • Satellite on land (on by default): the land around the spot drapes the map's satellite image (the one Terrain → Satellite uses) instead of the plain height colours.

On the map: the viewer's boat is shown with a coral pole, a ⛵ and a flat arrow on the water (about 2 km long, outlined) pointing along its heading. As it sails it moves across the map and lays a pale-yellow track with a breadcrumb dot every ~250 m and a ring at the start, so tacks and course changes show as turns. At real speed a boat covers only ~200 m a minute, invisible at map scale, so use the viewer's time selector (real time, ×10, ×60) to fast-forward the passage: the boat's progress, the land and the track run faster, while the waves, puffs and wind streaks stay real time. Reset position clears the track.

The spot it's showing is marked on the map with a coral pole and a ⛵ while it's open. It shows what that height and period look like, not the actual waves at that moment. It follows the time slider and playback: the same spot, redrawn for each hour. Its note adds the time, the sources and the NWS forecast for that zone and period.

Tidal currents

Sea & weather → Tidal currents (Salish Sea and Hawaiʻi): NOAA's tidal current predictions at its stations (about 170 on the Salish Sea; in Hawaiʻi a handful: Upolu Point, the Alalakeiki, ʻAuʻau, Pailolo and Kalohi channels, Lahaina, Hawea Point, Lāʻau Point), for the time on screen. Swinomish Channel (no NOAA station): three stations along the channel estimated from NOAA's La Conner tide predictions with the local rule of thumb (Port of Skagit and tug operators): slack comes 2 to 3.5 hours after high or low water at La Conner (about 2 h on a small change, 3.5 h on a big one), so the current sets north round high water and south round low; up to about 2 kt on a mean change, more on a big one. Other versions put slack 2.5 to 4 h after, and river runoff shifts it: allow an hour either way. They're labelled "estimated". Between stations the currents are blended on the planner's 200 m water, so narrow channels (Deception Pass, Agate Passage, Port Townsend Canal) carry their stations' currents. An arrow at each station points where the current sets, longer and warmer-coloured the faster it runs (blue under 1 kt to red over 4 kt).

  • Each way of drawing it on its own, with its own opacity (the slider on its row): Colour fill (on): the current's speed everywhere on the water as a colour wash, blue under 1 kt through green and yellow to red over 4 kt (key on the map). With SSCOFS it's the model's own current; with the stations it's a smooth blend of the stations within 5 km that can "see" the spot across water (a station never reaches across a point or an island), fading out with distance from the nearest. Arrows: on a ~2.5 km grid (the same field), or one at each NOAA station, or none; Arrow size. Particles (on): drifting with the current over the whole domain, sped up so it reads (about 2 kt crosses 8% of the view a second), placed and paced for the view; density (3,000 at 1×) and flow speed. Streamlines (off): evenly spaced with arrowheads, eddies included, traced again once the view stops moving. Stations: small rings, dots coloured by the station's own current, or not shown (click one for its current graph).
  • The fill, arrows, particles and streamlines all come from the current on a 400 m grid at each forecast hour, blended between the two hours either side of the time on screen on the graphics card: playing the forecast flows smoothly and costs little (the hour after is prepared in an idle moment). The fill is clipped to the chart's 200 m shoreline.
  • Label primary stations: the names and speeds of NOAA's harmonic (reference) stations, thinned so they don't overlap.
  • Click a station (a ring, arrow or dot) for its current graph: the current through the loaded run like a meteogram, flood above the line (with its direction) and ebb below, slack water and the maximum flood and ebb marked with their speeds, and the time on screen as a yellow line; the note gives the current now and the next event. Click the graph to move the time slider there.
  • Flag wind against current (on): a ring where the current runs against the wind and makes the local chop steep (amber: against a current over ~0.3 kt, 40% or more steeper than it would be, and over ~1 ft) or dangerous (red: breaking-steep, or the current stops the waves, and over ~2.5 ft). The panel lists the worst with the numbers: current, wind, chop before and after, wavelength and steepness.
  • Current effects on waves (on): the sea state colours, hover readout and surface viewer include the current (from the field between the stations; farther than 5 km from one, none). The chop grows in the wind over the moving water, and is then shortened and steepened against the current (higher and shorter, capped where it breaks) or lengthened with it, from wave–current theory (Longuet-Higgins & Stewart). Swell is left as it is (long waves change little).
  • What they are: NOAA's harmonic and subordinate station predictions (slack and maximum flood and ebb, with NOAA's usual sine curve between), stored with the site a year at a time (pipeline.currents): tides only, no wind-driven or river currents, and only at the stations; currents change sharply round points and in passes, so between stations it's an estimate. The flood and ebb directions are each station's mean.

Passage planner

Passage tab. Plan a passage through waypoints: Add waypoints on the map, then click the water for the start, any points to pass and the end. Drag a pin to move it; in the list, ↑ ↓ reorder and × removes one (or Undo, Clear). Reverse turns the list round for the trip back; Best order puts the waypoints between the start and the end in the order with the shortest way by water. Round within (0.2 nm): an intermediate waypoint counts as passed anywhere within that distance, as you'd round a mark (0: touch the point; the start and end are always exact). The planner's water is a 200 m mask built from the NOAA chart's own depth areas (the ENC behind the chart layer, harbour and coastal scale), worked out at 50 m inside each cell so channels narrower than a cell stay open (Agate Passage, Port Townsend Canal, Deception Pass, the Gig Harbor entrance, Pole and Obstruction Passes, and the dredged Swinomish Channel); drying flats and charted spits and jetties count as land, and the GSHHS coastline is used only where there's no US chart (Canadian waters). The distance off land is measured from the charted shore; a point at the shore that falls on land is moved to the nearest water (the status says how far), and the clearance is waived within 1.5 km of each waypoint so a harbour start can get out.

Common passages: pick From, an optional Via and To (alphabetical) and Set waypoints. Salish Sea: marinas, harbours and anchorages from Olympia and Tacoma to Bellingham, Blaine, the San Juans, Sidney and Victoria, Port Angeles, Sequim Bay and Neah Bay; vias include Port Townsend Canal, Admiralty Inlet (off Bush Point or Marrowstone Point), Deception Pass, Agate and Rich Passages, Colvos and East Passages, the Tacoma Narrows, Cattle Pass, Thatcher Pass, and Haro and Rosario Straits. Hawaiʻi: Honokōhau Small Boat Harbor, Makalawena, Nishimura Bay, the Kona and Kohala harbours, Hāna, the Maui, Lānaʻi and Molokaʻi harbours, a turning point off Hāna and the expedition route's turnaround off Kīpahulu (on the ʻUpolu–Kīpahulu line, 1 nm off Maui); vias are the channels, off ʻUpolu Point, off Hāna, the Kīpahulu turnaround, and the offshore waypoints from the owner's places (off Keāhole, Pūhili and Kawili Points and Cape Hanamanioa). Positions are approximate: planning, not navigation. Reset to defaults (by the heading) puts every planner setting back as it started; the waypoints and any wind already loaded are kept.

Two ways to plan:

  • With the forecast (wind, waves, currents): the fastest route through the loaded model run, leaving at the Depart time. The router uses the standard weather-routing method (isochrones): from the start, every 10 minutes (the time step), it tries headings every 5° round the course, with the boat's speed from its polars at the true wind angle in the wind there and then (the model's 10 m wind, hour by hour, blended between hours), less the speed lost to the waves on that heading (the chop from the wind over the water, steepened against a current, and the swell), and leeway on the wind; plus the current's set and drift. It keeps the most promising positions (the ones with least distance to go by water, round the land) and steps on until one reaches the next waypoint. A tack or gybe costs time (Tack costs; gybes 60% of it). Near the start it may wait in harbour for a fair tide or a better wind when that gets there sooner than sailing about. Along the way it may hold for the tide (anchored, or standing off out of the stream): at the approach to a tide gate (narrow water under ~0.5 nm on the way, including a pass at a via waypoint, not the start and end harbours), or wherever a strong current (0.8 kt or more) is foul along the way within ~2 nm of shore, never in the narrows themselves, up to about a tide (7 h) at a time and as often as it pays. Holding and pushing on are both carried forward and whichever arrives first wins, so it pushes through against a foul current when the boat still makes good progress, and holds when waiting for the turn gets there sooner; a plan can go, hold, go and hold again. Holds show in the results ("Hold for the tide": how long, where, from when), in "why this route", the table, the map tag and the route report's cautions (numbered on its map).
    • Limits (sliders; each move ending past one is ruled out): max gust (30 kt), max seas (combined chop and swell, 8 ft), off land (0.5 nm clearance, waived near your waypoints, which are often in harbours), and avoid dangerous wind-against-current (the red rings). Where your clearance would close the passes the natural way goes through (the way by water would be more than ~15% longer), it's eased only within ~0.8 nm of those narrow places (to half, 0.1 or 0.05 nm), and your clearance holds everywhere else on the leg; the status names the places (e.g. "near Agate Passage") and says what the full clearance would cost. If nothing gets through, it says what stopped it most, where and when, suggests which limit to ease, and shows the route as far as it got; a search that makes no progress for 6 hours (other than waiting in harbour) stops and says so.
    • Currents from (Salish Sea): SSCOFS model (the default) or NOAA stations. SSCOFS is NOAA's Salish Sea operational forecast model (FVCOM, a mesh down to tens of metres in the channels): the surface current everywhere on the water, with back-eddies, the channels and the effects of wind and rivers, out to 72 h from its newest run (SSCOFS runs at 03, 09, 15 and 21Z; the shared Salish Sea currents data at fp-sea.github.io/salish-currents is refreshed once a day, each morning from the 09Z run, the same data the Salish Sea Flow Explorer uses); past its hours, or outside its area, the NOAA station predictions take over. Each run is also checked against NOAA's predictions at every station (the shared data's skill file): SSCOFS agrees at most of them, but in a few constrictions too narrow for its mesh it runs far too weak — about a third of NOAA's predicted ebb in Deception Pass narrows, also Guemes Channel, Pickering Passage, Totten Inlet. Within about 1.2 km of a station where the run is that far off, the planner uses NOAA's predictions instead, and the current source says which stations. The stations: NOAA's tidal predictions at its stations, blended between them (a year ahead, tide only). The status and the report say which was used and how old the SSCOFS run is. Checked against NOAA's predictions at Admiralty Inlet, Point Wilson, Deception Pass, Rosario and Haro Straits and the Tacoma Narrows: the same directions and similar speeds. The report's station table adds SSCOFS's current at each station as the boat passes, beside NOAA's.
    • Motor through narrow channels (on, water narrower than 0.25 nm): no room to sail or tack, so the boat motors at the motoring speed through them (Agate Passage, Port Townsend Canal, Deception Pass, the Swinomish Channel, tight marina and harbour entrances), following the channel's bends, even with light-air motoring off; the map tag and the route hover say "narrow channel".
    • Currents (on where there are stations) and Motor in light air (off). When on: Motor when sailing speed would be under (3 kn) is a boat speed, not a wind speed: the engine goes on where the boat can't make that speed on any point of sail in the wind there (its best speed under sail, after the waves), and off again when it can. In a breeze it sails and tacks to windward rather than motoring into it. Motoring speed (5.5 kn) is your speed through the water under power, a little less in head seas.
    • Results: departure and ETA (local and UTC), passage time, distance sailed against the straight line, average speed and speed made good, the strongest wind and gust and the biggest seas met, the tacks and gybes, time motoring, any wait and what for (gusts near your limit, a fair tide, better wind), time with a fair or foul current (and the worst foul current), and the hours of darkness with sunrise and sunset on the way. Why this route: a few plain sentences on what shaped it (land in the way, beating and tacking, waiting, the current's net help, a limit it runs up against, motoring). Hover the route line on the map for what the boat is doing there: point of sail; STW and SOG; HDG, COG and CMG (course made good from the leg's start); TWS (and gust) and the direction it's from; TWA and the tack; AWS and AWA; the current, seas and sails. A °True / °Magnetic switch (in the replay bar) sets every bearing: the hover, the map tag, the "now" line and the table's COG (magnetic uses a fixed variation for the region, 15.2° E Salish Sea, 9.6° E Hawaiʻi, approximate for 2026: check the chart's compass rose). Export: GPX route (turn points: start, tacks and gybes, real course changes, your waypoints, end, with ETAs; for Navionics, Garmin, Raymarine, B&G, OpenCPN, iNavX and others), GPX track (the full planned track with times) and CSV (turn points with latitude, longitude, ETA, course and distance to the next). Sensitivity (with currents on, also: the currents 25% weaker and stronger, the tide turning 30 minutes earlier and later, and on the Salish Sea the other current source, SSCOFS or the stations; automatic, in the background, with Sensitivity automatically on: it starts after each route using only the forecast already loaded, its progress shows under the route, and changing the route stops it; cases needing more forecast say so, with Run in full; the button runs it in full; the PDF always has it in full): the same passage routed again (at the plan's own resolution) with one thing changed at a time: the wind 15% lighter and stronger, the boat sailing 10% and 20% slower and faster, without the currents (no drift, and no current steepening the chop), and leaving an hour at a time up to 6 h later, and earlier only while that's still ahead of now and inside the forecast (a departure in the past makes no sense). The NWS zone comparison

The forecast text is split into zones and periods (Tonight, Monday, Mon Night …), and each period into wind, seas (or "waves" in the inland zones) and the "Wave Detail" trains. The text gives period names, not times, so each is placed with the usual split (day 6 am–6 pm, night 6 pm–6 am, local time), starting from the issue time. The period covering the time on screen is the one shown.

Each forecast train is labelled chop or swell:

  • Chop when its period is 6 s or less, or when it comes from within 45° of that period's wind with a period that wind could raise (up to 0.6 U + 2 s, U the top wind in m/s).
  • Swell otherwise.

The model's side is taken over the zone's water (every 1 km cell inside its outline):

  • Wind: the 10 m wind.
  • Seas: our total, chop and swell combined.
  • Chop: our chop, with its peak period.
  • Swell: NOAA's trains near the zone's centre.

Each is given as typical (median) · 1 in 10 above (90th percentile). The flag compares the typical value with the forecaster's range: ≈ within it give or take 20–25% (at least 3 kt or 1 ft), ▼ well under, ▲ well over. NWS "seas" are the significant height of all the waves together, the same measure as our total.

Fog and mist

From the model's surface visibility:

  • Fog is under 1 km; mist is 1–5 km.
  • Density: full at 200 m visibility or less, easing off towards 5 km.
  • Depth: the model's cloud water from the ground up (to 600 m). Where visibility is low but there's no cloud water at the surface (haze, drizzle), a shallow 60 m layer is drawn.

The note gives how much of the water has fog and mist, and the typical fog depth.

Visibility

The same visibility forecast as a flat map, in the classes the UK Met Office's shipping forecast uses:

  • Very poor: under 1,000 m (fog).
  • Poor: 1,000 m to 2 nm.
  • Moderate: 2–5 nm.
  • Good: over 5 nm (left clear).

Hover for the value in nautical miles and metres. The model's visibility drops for anything that blocks the view (fog, mist, haze, smoke, rain or snow) and doesn't say which. The fog layer's depth and the satellite help tell them apart.

Particles

Particles ride the wind interpolated to their position, in a terrain-following height coordinate: near the ground they follow the terrain, and above about 6 km the coordinate is flat. Speeds are to scale: every particle moves at its wind speed times one factor for the region (on the Salish Sea, 1 s on screen ≈ 14 minutes of real motion). Rising and sinking are also multiplied by the vertical exaggeration and the optional boost. The particle box states the factor and shows a 10 kt reference streak.

Near the ground the height coordinate already follows the terrain, so a particle's height changes only by the part of w that isn't the air following the ground; the boost applies to that part, so air hugging a slope isn't lifted off it. With w now anchored at the ground, particles in the lowest few hundred metres rise and sink too (before, they had no vertical motion below the lowest pressure level).

Each particle lives 3–8 seconds and fades in and out, then starts again somewhere new. One that has to stop early (it reached land with "over water only" on, left its slab, or ran off the model grid) fades out where it is instead of vanishing. Over a coloured slice, particles are drawn as dark "ink" streaks, so the slice carries the value and the streaks the direction; elsewhere they glow in their own colours.

8. Tips and workflows

Will it blow in the channel this afternoon? Choose "Surface wind & gusts", switch Colour by to Surface gust, and play through the afternoon. Hover the channel exit. Turn on observed wind at stations and set the time to now to see how the model compares with the buoys right now.
How deep is the gap flow? Choose "Gap flow: along the channel". The cross-section down the axis shows the fast core and how high it goes; the particle slab shows the flow. Slide the section across with [ ]. Open Channel analysis for the cap depth and Froude number along the channel.
Is there an inversion capping the trades? Choose "Inversion & moisture". The violet band is the capping layer; hover it for base and top. Compare its height with the mountains: peaks above it block the flow beneath.
How rough is it out there? Turn on Sea state and play through the day. Chop builds downwind of long open reaches: the eastern Strait in a westerly, the north end of Puget Sound in a southerly, the ʻAlenuihāhā exit in the trades. Click a spot to see it at true scale. Compare the period with the height: short, steep chop (3–4 s) is much rougher to be in than the same height at a longer period.
Does the model agree with the forecaster? Turn on Sea state and pick your zone under "NWS zone". Step through the forecast: the table follows the NWS period for each hour. A ▼ on chop with ≈ on wind often means the sea has been building longer than the "wind for" setting: try 24 h. A ▼ on swell usually means the forecaster expects more from a distant storm than the wave model has.
Is the model's cloud right? Turn on Clouds and Satellite, choose clean infrared (day or night), and look from above ("Above" view). Toggle Clouds off and on to see which is which: grey-blue patches are the satellite, white volumes the model. Scrub back to hour 0–2 of the run, which the satellite has, to compare at the same time. The model placing the cloud bands right but missing small puffs is normal (see "Clouds" in section 9).
Will there be fog on the water? Turn on Fog & visibility and play through the night into the morning. Fog forming over the water near the coast overnight and lifting by late morning is the classic pattern. Add the Satellite at dawn: fog and low stratus look smooth and flat in visible imagery, and are hard to see in infrared at night because they're about as warm as the sea.

9. Learning the weather behind it

Gap winds

When air is pushed toward a mountain barrier that has a gap, it accelerates through the gap. The main driver is the pressure difference along the gap: air flows from high pressure to low, faster the steeper the drop. Stable air that can't easily rise over the mountains is funnelled through instead. Winds are usually strongest at or just beyond the exit, where the flow fans out.

Try it: in the ʻAlenuihāhā on a trade-wind day, compare the 10 m wind upstream (north-east of the channel) with the exit (south-west). The channel analysis gives the speed-up ratio; around ×1.6 is typical.

The capping inversion

Normally temperature falls with height. In an inversion it rises, which makes a lid: air below can't easily mix upward. Hawaiʻi's trade-wind inversion sits around 2 km. It keeps the trade-wind clouds shallow, and it keeps the wind in a layer the mountains can block and the channels can squeeze.

Try it: "Inversion & moisture", then a cross-section across Hawaiʻi Island. The humidity drops sharply above the violet band.

Potential temperature and stability

Potential temperature (θ) is the temperature air would have if brought down to 1000 mb. If θ increases with height, the air is stable: displaced parcels return, and vertical motion is suppressed. If it decreases, the air is unstable and overturns. Inversions are layers where θ jumps up quickly.

Froude number

It compares how fast the air moves with how fast a disturbance (a wave on top of the capped layer, or the air's response to a mountain) can travel. Below 1, the flow "feels" obstacles ahead and tends to go around. Above 1, it can't adjust in time: it thins and speeds up, and later "jumps" back. See the Channel analysis page.

Richardson number and turbulence

Stability damps turbulence; wind shear creates it. Their ratio, the Richardson number, says which wins. Below about 0.25, shear wins and layers break into waves and eddies (Kelvin–Helmholtz). That's where bumpy air aloft and gusty mixing near the surface come from.

Vorticity and eddies

Vorticity measures spin about the vertical. Behind islands and headlands, flow separates into eddies that show as paired positive and negative vorticity, like the counter-rotating eddies in the lee of Hawaiʻi Island. They can reverse the wind near the coast.

Try it: "Eddies & rotation", looking at the lee (west) of Hawaiʻi Island.

Vertical motion

Air rises over windward slopes (clouds, rain) and sinks in the lee (clearing, drying, and gusts brought down from aloft). Vertical speeds are small, centimetres to a metre per second, which is why the explorer can exaggerate them.

Try it: "Up & down drafts". Blue is sinking and red is rising.

Convergence zones

Where surface winds run into each other, the air has nowhere to go but up: convergence. The rising air cools, and if it's moist it makes a band of cloud and showers. The Puget Sound Convergence Zone is the classic local case. After a front passes, westerly to north-westerly winds off the Pacific split around the Olympic Mountains. One branch comes down the Strait of Juan de Fuca and turns south; the other comes up through the Chehalis gap and turns north up the Sound. They meet over the central Sound and Snohomish County, usually between Seattle and Everett. The band is typically 15–30 km wide and can stall for hours, with heavy showers under it and sunshine either side.

Try it: when the Seattle forecast discussion mentions a convergence zone, load the long run, choose "Convergence & lift" and play through the evening. Look for a red band across the Sound or over Snohomish County with the 10 m winds meeting it from north and south, and the particles in it rising. Model updrafts in these bands are weak (0.1–0.5 m/s), which is why the preset boosts vertical motion ×10.

Gusts and the boundary layer

Near the surface, friction and heating stir the air into a mixed layer. Gusts are faster air from higher in that layer brought down by eddies. A deep mixed layer, strong winds aloft and sinking air make for higher gusts. The gust factor (gust ÷ mean wind) of 1.3–1.5 or more signals gusty, turbulent flow.

Vertical mixing, stability and the lid

Mixing is the air being stirred up and down, by the wind rubbing over the surface and by heating from below. The stirred layer next to the surface is the mixed layer (the model's boundary layer). Inside it, fast air from higher up is carried down in eddies: that's what a gust is. So the wind that can reach you on the water isn't just the 10 m wind: it's anything up to the strongest wind in the mixed layer. A deep mixed layer under strong winds aloft makes for gusty, lumpy sailing; a shallow one keeps the surface wind to itself.

Stability decides how deep the stirring goes. Over the water the quickest read is sea minus air temperature. When the sea is warmer than the air (+, red), the air is heated from below and rises: it's unstable, the mixing is vigorous, the wind aloft comes down in gusts, showers pop up, the sea gets confused. Typical behind a cold front, in cold northerly outbreaks, and on winter afternoons on the Salish Sea. When the air is warmer than the sea (−, blue) it's stable: the surface layer is cut off from the wind above, so the surface wind is smoother and often lighter than the wind a few hundred metres up, and warm moist air over cold water makes fog (spring and summer on the Salish Sea, the outer coast, the Strait). Within about ±1 °C it's near neutral: the wind's own stirring (strong wind, rough sea) sets the mixing.

The lid: an inversion. An inversion (warmer air above cooler) stops rising air, so the mixed layer reaches up to it and no further. They're not the same thing: mixing is the stirring, the inversion is the ceiling on it. In Hawaiʻi the trade-wind inversion sits at about 1.5–2.5 km: the trades blow in the layer beneath it, which is why the mountains can block them and the channels squeeze them (the ʻAlenuihāhā), and why the trade cumulus stay shallow. On the Salish Sea in summer the marine inversion sits much lower, a few hundred metres, capping a cool, moist marine layer (fog and stratus, the westerly push through the Strait). A low lid means shallow mixing and less wind brought down; a lid that lifts or breaks (daytime heating over land, a front) lets the mixing deepen and the afternoon get gustier.

Reading it here. The Vertical mixing & gusts preset colours the map by the wind in the mixed layer, with barbs for the 10 m wind: where the colour is much stronger than the barbs, gusts can bring that down (on the Salish Sea often 1.3–1.5× the mean in unstable air). Hover the water for the Mixing line: "Mixing: mixed layer 800 m (2,600 ft) · up to 24 kt in it (15 kt at 10 m) · sea 2.1 °C warmer than the air: unstable, the wind aloft mixes down (gusty)". Switch Colour by to Mixed-layer depth (how deep the stirring goes) or Sea minus air temperature (the Stability over the water preset). The Inversion lid & mixed layer preset shows the two together in a cross-section: the inversion band as the ceiling, the mixed layer's top beneath it, turbulence-proxy particles in between. Gust factor (gust ÷ mean) is the model's own answer at the surface; the mixing fields show why.

Rules of thumb for the water. Unstable (sea warmer) + a deep mixed layer + strong wind aloft: expect gusts well above the forecast mean and sudden shifts; reef early. Stable (air warmer) + a shallow mixed layer: the surface wind can be much lighter than the wind at mast-head height or on the hills, and it can arrive suddenly when the stable layer is scoured out (at a headland, the mouth of a pass, or when the sun heats the land). Near the coast, the land mixes by day (deep over hot land, shallow over the cold water): afternoon sea breezes and gusty wind off the shore.

Limits. The mixed-layer depth is the model's diagnosis (its turbulence scheme's definition), good to within a few hundred metres; over cold water it's often very shallow and noisy. The skin temperature over water is the model's sea-surface analysis, updated slowly: it misses tidal mixing in the passes (cold, well-mixed water at Deception Pass, Admiralty Inlet and Haro Strait) and local upwelling. The wind in the mixed layer is a ceiling for gusts, not a forecast of each gust. The light HRRR has winds at 10 and 80 m and 1000, 925, 850, 700 mb, so its mixed-layer wind is coarser aloft than the full column's.

Clouds and the inversion

Air rising over the sea cools until it saturates, and cloud forms. In the trades, the inversion stops that rise, so trade cumulus grow only up to the lid and spread along it; their tops sit just under the inversion base. Mountains force the air up, so cloud banks up on windward slopes and clears in the lee, where the air sinks and warms.

Try it: Clouds with the Inversion layer on in Hawaiʻi. The cloud tops meet the violet band.

Fog

Fog is a cloud at the ground. Around here it forms two main ways:

  • Advection fog: moist marine air moves over colder water, as with the Pacific marine layer pushing down the Strait of Juan de Fuca in summer.
  • Radiation fog: the ground and the air next to it cool on clear, calm nights, typically in valleys and over the Sound in fall and winter, burning off after sunrise.

Fog is shallow, often under 100–300 m, and sensitive to small details: a degree of temperature, a slight breeze. Models find it hard to forecast.

Reading the satellite

  • Visible: shows what reflects sunlight. Thick cloud is bright, thin cloud grey, sea dark.
  • Infrared: shows temperature. High, cold cloud tops are brightest, so tall storms stand out, while low cloud and fog (about as warm as the ground) barely show.
  • GeoColor: blends them, true colour by day and infrared at night.

Compare with the model: where both have cloud, the model has the weather pattern right. Cloud only in the satellite often means small cumulus or thin cloud the model can't resolve.

How wind builds waves

Wind drags on the sea and passes energy to the waves. They grow in height and period until one of three things stops them:

  • they run out of fetch (the distance of open water upwind);
  • the wind hasn't blown long enough (duration);
  • they're "fully developed" for that wind speed.

Inland waters are nearly always fetch-limited, so the same 20 kt wind gives very different seas depending on direction. Chop is the local wind sea: short, steep, from the wind's direction. Swell is waves that have left the storm that made them: long, smooth, and able to come from a different direction. Wave energy grows with the square of height, and a wave's steepness is its height over its wavelength. A 3 ft chop at 3 s (wavelength about 14 m) is steep and uncomfortable; 3 ft of swell at 12 s (about 225 m) is a gentle heave.

Where swell comes from: distant storms. The North Pacific in winter sends west to north-west swell to both regions. The Southern Hemisphere in summer sends long south swell (14–20 s) to Hawaiʻi's south shores. The trades raise their own east-north-east wind sea. Long-period swell travels thousands of kilometres with little loss, and its period tells you how far it came: the longer the period, the more distant and stronger the storm.

Try it: Sea state on the Salish Sea. Set "wind for" to 6 h and then 24 h, and watch how much the open outer coast changes and how little the inland waters do.

Reading a wind barb

The staff points into the wind, the direction it comes from. Feathers are on the end away from the station: a half feather is 5 kt, a full one 10 kt, a triangle (pennant) 50 kt. A circle is calm.

10. Assumptions and limits

  • Resolution: 2.5–3 km grids resolve features several grid lengths across (roughly 10–20 km) well; narrower jets, eddies and gusts are smoothed or missing. Pressure levels are 25 mb apart (RRFS Hawaiʻi) or 50 mb in HRRR's default column (25 mb is an option), about 250–500 m near the surface, so thin layers can be blurred.
  • Below the lowest pressure level (roughly the lowest 100 m over the sea, more over land), w and vorticity are undefined; wind comes from the above-ground winds.
  • Proxies aren't measurements: turbulence classes, the gust factor and the Froude number are inferred from model profiles.
  • Near coasts, model winds within about one grid cell of land are partly land-influenced, even with "over water only".
  • Observations are single points at different heights (buoys ~4 m, stations often 10–20 m or more) from the model's 10 m; allow for that when comparing.
  • Canadian waters have no marine-chart data (the chart is NOAA's US charts).
  • Model pressure gradients in the Strait can be too weak (about 30% in one well-studied case), and with them the gap winds.
  • No bias correction: the explorer shows the model as it is.
  • Clouds are grid-scale: the model's cloud water forms only where a whole 2.5–3 km cell saturates. Small cumulus narrower than that (many trade-wind puffs) are missed or spread into broad patches. The partial cloudiness the model accounts for internally isn't in these fields.
  • TKE is HRRR-only and approximate in height: it comes from the model's native levels, placed with a fixed height table measured once; the turbulence jitter is an illustration scaled by it.
  • Polars are estimates: neither boat has a published measured polar here; the tables are reasoned from hull type and waterline. Real polars can replace them (web/polars.js). The boat's speed doesn't include the waves' effect or current.
  • Gusts are illustrations of the model's numbers: the model gives a gust estimate, not individual gusts, so the inspector's puffs and the particles' gustiness follow its gust strength with made-up timing and spacing. Rising and sinking air is the model's resolved vertical motion (terrain, fronts, convergence, sea breezes); turbulent eddies are too small for the model to resolve and aren't in the vertical motion.
  • Raster charts lie flat at sea level: the chart's land shows only where the 3D terrain is lower than the chart layer (a narrow strip at the coast). The stored NOAA chart is as of the date in its note; the live one needs NOAA's server to be up, and its zoomed-in image loads about half a second after you stop moving. Chart 19320 is a fixed 2024 copy.
  • Convergence is sensitive: derivatives of the wind amplify small-scale noise. It's smoothed to about 12 km, but over rough terrain the 10 m field still has valley and ridge patterns; read broad bands, not single cells. A slice a few hundred metres up is often cleaner.
  • NWS periods are approximate: the forecast gives period names, not times, so the day/night boundaries (6 am, 6 pm) are assumed. The text is read as written: an unusual phrasing may be missed (that row then shows "–"). The forecast is for the zone as a whole, while the model gives values everywhere; its range is compared with the model's typical value.
  • Fog is a diagnosis: the model's visibility is a rough estimate. It also includes haze and precipitation, and fog forecasts in general are uncertain.
  • Satellite position: the image is laid flat at sea level. GOES-West sees the Salish Sea at a slant, so high cloud tops appear displaced several km away from the satellite (to the north-east there); low cloud and fog sit close to the right place. The image also lags real time by 20–40 minutes, and only past times can be matched.
  • Swell is coarse: the wave model's cells are about 18 km, so it doesn't resolve channels, headlands or island shadows finer than that. Near coasts the nearest sea cell is used; sheltered waters get none. Combining it with the chop assumes the two are independent.
  • Chop leaves out a lot:
    • tidal currents: a current against the wind steepens and heightens the waves sharply, as in the Strait of Juan de Fuca, Rosario Strait, Admiralty Inlet and the Tacoma Narrows;
    • shallow water and wave refraction around headlands;
    • waves from other places.
    It also treats the wind at each point as the wind along its whole fetch, and assumes a steady wind for the chosen duration. Read it as the typical local wind sea, especially in and near tide rips.
  • Balloon soundings come from the IEM archive, which can lag; a sounding over 2 days old is named but not compared.
